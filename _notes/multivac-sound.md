Your last instinct is the right one, and it's worth seeing why the others lose to it.

The fake-distribution approach would work but it's solving the wrong problem — you'd be hand-crafting a statistical impression of an event stream that your simulator already produces for real. The relaxation cascade is a natural choreography: an input flips, some relays switch in iteration 1, their contacts cause others to switch in iteration 2, and so on down the settle depth of 1–7. That's not simultaneous clicking — it's a ripple. Map each cascade iteration to a few milliseconds of audio time, add per-relay random jitter (real relays vary ±20% mechanically, so nothing ever lands on the exact same sample), and you get the authentic sound of relay machines for free: not "click," but that rolling krrr-CHUNK — clock relays firing first, then a wavefront of logic clattering after them, dense when many bits change, sparse when few do. A line clear in Tetris would sound like a downpour; a piece drifting sideways would be a patter. The sound becomes a live oscilloscope — you'd literally hear a stuck loop or a huge settle cascade as a texture change. That's the alluring part, and no faked distribution reproduces it, because the information is the timing.

And playing them all isn't insane — that intuition comes from imagining 1000 independent sound players. Audio doesn't work that way: mixing is summing. Air does it, mixing desks do it, and your DAC receives one waveform no matter what. A relay click is a ~20–50 ms transient; the cheap trick is to render, not perform: on each simulation event, collect the list of (relay, cascade-iteration) changes, schedule each as (time offset + jitter, amplitude, sample variant) into a single audio buffer, sum, and play the buffer. Summing a few hundred short samples into a buffer is trivial arithmetic — your 1 ms simulator will outrun it comfortably. Record or synthesize maybe 6–10 click variants (or one click pitch-shifted a few percent per relay, seeded by relay ID so each relay keeps a consistent "voice"), pan each machine to a fixed stereo position so the cabinet has spatial width, and vary amplitude slightly by which relay it is.

Two perceptual facts make this even easier than it sounds. First, beyond roughly 20–30 near-simultaneous transients, the ear stops counting and hears density — so mass is conveyed automatically, no representative-sample trickery needed. Second, when two clicks do land within a fraction of a millisecond, their sum is simply one louder click — which is exactly what a real cabinet does acoustically. Your "loud sound representing 50 relays" idea is thus what naive summation already produces, minus the artifice. If you ever hit a pathological event with thousands of changes, the graceful degradation is a voice cap: keep the loudest N, fold the rest into a shaped noise bed. But at 600–900 relays you likely never need it.

So: no fakery, no full physical audio sim — just event-driven scheduling with cascade-derived timing and per-relay jitter, rendered by summation. It's the least work of your four options and the only one where the sound is causally true, which means it stays interesting forever instead of being a loop you eventually notice.
